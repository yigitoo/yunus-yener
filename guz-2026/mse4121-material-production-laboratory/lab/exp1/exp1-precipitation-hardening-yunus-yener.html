1) Aim of the Experiment

The aim of this experiment is to apply the three steps of precipitation hardening (solution treatment, quenching and artificial aging) to a heat-treatable 7xxx series (Al–Zn–Mg) aluminium alloy and to follow how its Brinell hardness changes with aging time. From the hardness–time curve, the under-aged, peak-aged and over-aged regions are identified and the results are compared with the literature.

2) Theoretical Information

2.1 Why precipitates make a metal harder

A metal deforms plastically when dislocations glide on their slip planes. Anything placed in the path of a moving dislocation raises the stress needed to keep it moving, and therefore raises strength and hardness. Small second-phase particles distributed through the grains are one of the most effective obstacles. In precipitation hardening these particles are not added from outside: they are grown inside the alloy by a controlled heat treatment, so the chemical composition stays the same while the mechanical properties change.

2.2 Conditions and the three steps

An alloy can be precipitation hardened only if the solubility of the alloying element in the matrix decreases with decreasing temperature (a sloping solvus line in the phase diagram). The 2xxx (Al–Cu), 6xxx (Al–Mg–Si) and 7xxx (Al–Zn–Mg) aluminium alloys satisfy this condition.

  1. Solution treatment: the alloy is heated into the single-phase α region, just below the solidus, so that the alloying elements dissolve completely in the aluminium matrix.
  2. Quenching: rapid cooling in water does not give the atoms time to diffuse and form the equilibrium phase. The result is a supersaturated solid solution (αss), which also contains excess quenched-in vacancies that later speed up diffusion.
  3. Aging: the supersaturated solution is held at room temperature (natural aging) or at an elevated temperature below the solvus (artificial aging, ~100–300 °C). The excess solute leaves the matrix step by step as fine precipitates.

2.3 Precipitation sequence in 7xxx alloys: GP zones → metastable η′ → stable η

αss  →  GP zones  →  η′ (metastable)  →  η (MgZn2, stable)

  • GP (Guinier–Preston) zones: clusters of Zn and Mg atoms only a few atomic layers thick. They have the same lattice as the matrix and are fully coherent; the lattice planes run continuously through them, but the size mismatch creates elastic strain fields around them.
  • η′ phase (metastable): forms from the GP zones as aging continues. It has its own crystal structure but is still semi-coherent with the matrix. When η′ particles are fine and densely distributed, the alloy reaches its maximum hardness (peak aging).
  • η phase, MgZn2 (stable): the equilibrium phase. It is incoherent (it has a real interface with the matrix) and grows coarse at the expense of the smaller particles.

2.4 Coherency, particle size and the dislocation mechanism

While the precipitates are small and coherent, a dislocation can cut (shear) through them; the resistance comes from the coherency strain field and from the new interface created by cutting, and it increases as the particles grow. Once the particles become large and incoherent, cutting is no longer possible and dislocations bypass them by bowing between them and leaving a loop behind (Orowan mechanism). The stress for bypassing decreases as the spacing between particles increases. Peak hardness is reached at the particle size where these two mechanisms cross over. Further aging makes the particles coarser and fewer, the spacing increases, and the hardness falls: this is over-aging.

2.5 Precipitation hardening vs. dispersion hardening

In dispersion hardening the hard particles (for example oxides such as Al2O3) are insoluble in the matrix and are introduced by powder metallurgy rather than precipitated from a solid solution. They are incoherent from the beginning, act only as Orowan obstacles and do not dissolve or coarsen at high temperature. Precipitation-hardened alloys therefore reach higher room-temperature strength, but lose it if they are used above their aging temperature, whereas dispersion-hardened alloys keep their strength at high temperature.

Figure 1. Flow chart of the precipitation hardening experiment. (*hardness was measured on previously prepared samples)

  1. Solution treatment. The tube furnace was heated from 113 °C to the solution treatment temperature of 480 °C in about 10 minutes. The samples were placed in the furnace and held for 15, 30, 45 and 60 minutes, so that the effect of holding time on dissolution could be seen. Reason: at 480 °C the Zn and Mg atoms dissolve in the α-Al matrix and the alloy becomes a single-phase solid solution.
  2. Quenching. At the end of each holding time the sample was taken out as quickly as possible and plunged into water. Reason: the fast cooling prevents diffusion, so the solute atoms stay trapped in the lattice and a supersaturated solid solution forms. Any delay between furnace and water would let coarse precipitates form at the grain boundaries and reduce the later hardening.
  3. Artificial aging. The quenched samples were placed in the drying oven for artificial aging. Reason: the elevated temperature gives the solute atoms enough mobility to cluster and form GP zones and η′ precipitates.
  4. Hardness measurement. The laboratory time was not enough to complete the aging of the samples treated during our session, so their hardness could not be compared. Instead, hardness was measured on previously prepared samples of the same alloy that had been solution treated, quenched and aged for 0, 1, 3, 6 and 12 hours. Each sample was indented with the Brinell tester and the diameter of the indentation (d) was measured. The measurement was done quickly, because time spent outside the oven at room temperature can change the hardness (natural aging).

6) Discussion and Evaluation of the Results

6.1 Shape of the aging curve

The as-quenched sample (0 h) has the lowest hardness, 79.54 HB, because the solute atoms are still dissolved in the matrix and give only solid-solution strengthening. After 1 h of aging the hardness rises to 109.50 HB (+37.7 %). In this under-aged stage GP zones and the first η′ particles nucleate; they are coherent and very fine, and their strain fields make dislocation glide more difficult.

The maximum hardness, 130.95 HB, was measured after 3 h, 64.6 % higher than the as-quenched state. At this point the alloy contains the largest number of fine, semi-coherent η′ precipitates, and the stress needed for dislocations to cut or bypass them is highest. Since the samples were measured only at 1, 3 and 6 h, the true peak lies somewhere between 1 and 6 h; 3 h is the best estimate the data allow.

Over-aging begins after the 3 h point. At 6 h the hardness has already dropped to 124.24 HB (−5.1 % from the peak) and at 12 h to 94.95 HB (−27.5 % from the peak). During this stage the larger precipitates grow while the smaller ones dissolve, η′ transforms into the incoherent equilibrium η (MgZn2) phase and the distance between particles increases. Dislocations can now pass the particles by the Orowan mechanism at a lower stress, so the alloy softens. Even after 12 h the hardness is still 19.4 % higher than the as-quenched value, which shows that some precipitation strengthening remains.

6.2 Comparison with the literature

The measured curve has the classic aging-curve shape described in the literature for heat-treatable Al alloys: a rapid rise, a single maximum and a gradual decrease [1–3]. For the 7075 alloy, a typical member of the 7xxx series, handbook values are about 60 HB in the annealed (O) condition and about 150 HB in the peak-aged T6 condition [4]. Our values (79.5 HB as-quenched, 131 HB at the peak) lie between these limits and the peak is somewhat lower than T6. Likely reasons:

  • The standard T6 treatment ages 7075 at a lower temperature (about 120 °C for ~24 h). Aging at a higher temperature moves the peak to a much shorter time, which is why our peak appears after only a few hours, but the precipitates are coarser and the peak hardness is lower.
  • The exact composition of the laboratory alloy is not known; a leaner Zn + Mg content gives fewer precipitates.
  • The test conditions differ (HBW 2.5/62.5 here, HBW 10/500 in handbooks), and each value comes from a single indentation, so a scatter of a few HB is expected.

6.3 Precipitation hardening in PH stainless steels

The same principle is used in precipitation-hardening (PH) stainless steels. In 17-4 PH (AISI 630), the steel is solution treated at about 1040 °C and cooled to form a martensitic matrix supersaturated in copper (Condition A). During aging, very fine copper-rich particles precipitate inside the martensite and harden it. The aging temperature selects the property balance [5, 6]:

  • H900 (≈ 482 °C, 1 h) gives the highest strength and hardness (peak-aged).
  • H1025, H1150 (≈ 552–621 °C, 4 h) are deliberately over-aged: the Cu particles coarsen, hardness and strength decrease, but toughness and stress-corrosion resistance improve.

This is the same hardness–time/temperature behaviour seen in our aluminium samples: a peak followed by softening due to particle coarsening. The difference is the matrix (martensite instead of α-Al) and the much higher process temperatures. Engineers sometimes choose an over-aged condition on purpose, as in 7075-T73 or 17-4 PH H1150, when toughness and corrosion resistance are more important than maximum strength.

7) Conclusion

  • Artificial aging increased the hardness of the 7xxx Al alloy from 79.54 HB (as-quenched) to a maximum of 130.95 HB after 3 h, an increase of 64.6 %.
  • The hardness increase is caused by fine, coherent/semi-coherent GP zones and η′ precipitates that obstruct dislocation motion.
  • After about 3 h the alloy over-ages: hardness decreased to 124.24 HB at 6 h and 94.95 HB at 12 h as the precipitates coarsened into incoherent η (MgZn2) and dislocations bypassed them by the Orowan mechanism.
  • The curve agrees in shape with the literature; the peak is lower than for 7075-T6, mainly because of the higher aging temperature.
