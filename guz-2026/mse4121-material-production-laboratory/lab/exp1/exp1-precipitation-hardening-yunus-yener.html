1) Aim

The aim of this experiment is to examine how precipitation hardening changes the mechanical properties of a heat-treatable aluminium alloy without changing its chemical composition. A 7xxx series (Al–Zn–Mg) alloy was solution treated, quenched and artificially aged; its Brinell hardness was measured after different aging times. From the hardness–time curve, the maximum hardness and the start of over-aging were determined and the results were compared with the literature.

2) Materials and Devices

  • Samples: 7xxx series (Al–Zn–Mg) aluminium alloy
  • Tube furnace: 480 °C, for solution treatment
  • Water bath: for quenching (sudden cooling)
  • Drying oven (etüv): 200 °C, for artificial aging
  • Brinell hardness tester: 2.5 mm tungsten carbide ball, 62.5 kgf load (HBW 2.5/62.5), with measuring optics for the indentation diameter

Figure 1. Flow chart of the experiment.

  1. Solution treatment: the tube furnace was heated from 113 °C to 480 °C in about 10 min. The samples were held at 480 °C for 15, 30, 45 and 60 min so that Zn and Mg dissolve completely in the α-Al matrix.
  2. Quenching: each sample was taken out quickly and cooled in water. Fast cooling prevents diffusion, so the solute atoms stay trapped and a supersaturated solid solution (αss) forms. The samples were then dried.
  3. Artificial aging: the samples were placed in the oven at 200 °C. At this temperature the solute atoms can diffuse and form GP zones and η′ precipitates.
  4. Hardness measurement: the lab time was not enough to age our own samples, so hardness was measured on previously prepared samples of the same alloy aged at 200 °C for 0, 1, 3, 6 and 12 h. After removal from the oven they were cooled rapidly and indented quickly, and the indentation diameter (d) was measured.

4.2 Precipitation sequence and strengthening mechanism

Precipitation hardening is possible only in alloys whose solubility decreases with decreasing temperature (sloping solvus), such as the 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx Al alloys. Quenching from the solution treatment temperature produces a supersaturated solid solution; during aging the excess Zn and Mg leave the matrix in the following order:

αss  →  GP zones  →  η′ (metastable)  →  η (MgZn2, stable)

StageCoherency with matrixSizeHow dislocations passEffect
GP zonesfully coherentfew atomic layerscut (shear)hardness rises
η′semi-coherentfine, densecut, highest resistancepeak hardness
η (MgZn2)incoherentcoarse, widely spacedbypass (Orowan loops)softening

Table 2. Stages of precipitation in 7xxx Al alloys.

Coherent particles strain the surrounding lattice, and a dislocation must also create new interface when it cuts them, so the resistance grows as the particles grow. When they become large and incoherent, cutting is no longer possible and dislocations bow out between them (Orowan mechanism); this needs less stress when the particles are far apart. The peak is therefore reached at the particle size where cutting changes to bypassing. Dispersion hardening is different: insoluble, incoherent particles (e.g. Al2O3) are added by powder metallurgy, act only as Orowan obstacles and do not coarsen, so the strength is kept at high temperature.

4.3 Evaluation of the hardness–time curve

The as-quenched sample has the lowest hardness (79.54 HB) because the solute is still dissolved and only solid-solution strengthening acts. After 1 h the hardness rises to 109.50 HB (+37.7 %) as GP zones form. The maximum hardness, 130.95 HB, is reached at 3 h (+64.6 %), when the fine η′ particles are most effective. Since the samples were measured only at 1, 3 and 6 h, the exact peak lies between 1 and 6 h, and 3 h is the best estimate. Over-aging starts after 3 h: hardness drops to 124.24 HB at 6 h (−5.1 % from the peak) and to 94.95 HB at 12 h (−27.5 %), as η′ coarsens and transforms into incoherent η. Even after 12 h the hardness is still 19.4 % above the as-quenched value. All d/D ratios (0.31–0.39) are within the 0.24–0.60 range required for a valid Brinell test.

4.4 Comparison with the literature

The curve shows the typical shape reported for heat-treatable Al alloys: a rapid rise, a single maximum and a gradual decrease [1–3]. For 7075, handbook values are about 60 HB in the annealed (O) and about 150 HB in the peak-aged T6 condition [4]. Our peak (131 HB) is lower and appears earlier because:

  • T6 aging of 7075 is done at about 120 °C for ~24 h; at 200 °C diffusion is much faster, so the peak comes within a few hours, but the precipitates are coarser and the peak hardness is lower.
  • The exact composition of the laboratory alloy is not known, and each value comes from a single indentation.

In industry an over-aged condition is sometimes chosen on purpose (e.g. 7075-T73): strength is a little lower, but resistance to stress-corrosion cracking is much better.

4.5 Precipitation hardening in PH stainless steels

The same principle is used in precipitation-hardening stainless steels such as 17-4 PH (AISI 630). The steel is solution treated at about 1040 °C and cooled to a martensitic matrix supersaturated in copper. During aging, very fine Cu-rich particles precipitate in the martensite. H900 (482 °C, 1 h) gives the peak hardness, while H1025–H1150 (552–621 °C, 4 h) are deliberately over-aged: the particles coarsen, hardness decreases, but toughness and corrosion resistance improve [5]. The behaviour is the same as in our aluminium samples; only the matrix and the temperatures are different.

5) Conclusion

  • Aging at 200 °C raised the hardness of the 7xxx Al alloy from 79.54 HB to a maximum of 130.95 HB at 3 h (+64.6 %).
  • The increase is caused by coherent GP zones and semi-coherent η′ precipitates that hinder dislocation motion.
  • After 3 h the alloy over-aged (124.24 HB at 6 h, 94.95 HB at 12 h) as the precipitates coarsened into incoherent η (MgZn2) and dislocations bypassed them by the Orowan mechanism.
  • The curve agrees in shape with the literature; the lower and earlier peak compared with 7075-T6 is explained by the higher aging temperature.
