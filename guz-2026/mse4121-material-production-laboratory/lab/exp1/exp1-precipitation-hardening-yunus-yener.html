1) Aim

To apply solution treatment, quenching and artificial aging to a 7xxx series (Al–Zn–Mg) aluminium alloy, to follow the change of Brinell hardness with aging time, and to identify the under-aged, peak-aged and over-aged regions on the hardness–time curve.

2) Materials and Devices

7xxx series aluminium alloy samples; tube furnace (480 °C) for solution treatment; water bath for quenching; drying oven (200 °C) for artificial aging; Brinell hardness tester with a 2.5 mm tungsten carbide ball and 62.5 kgf load (HBW 2.5/62.5).

Figure 1. Flow chart of the experiment.

  1. Solution treatment: the tube furnace was heated from 113 °C to 480 °C in about 10 min. The samples were held at 480 °C for 15, 30, 45 and 60 min so that Zn and Mg dissolve completely in the α-Al matrix.
  2. Quenching: each sample was taken out quickly and cooled in water. Fast cooling prevents diffusion, so the solute atoms stay trapped and a supersaturated solid solution (αss) forms. The samples were then dried.
  3. Artificial aging: the samples were placed in the oven at 200 °C. At this temperature the solute atoms can diffuse and form GP zones and η′ precipitates.
  4. Hardness measurement: the lab time was not enough to age our own samples, so hardness was measured on previously prepared samples of the same alloy aged at 200 °C for 0, 1, 3, 6 and 12 h. After removal from the oven they were cooled rapidly and indented quickly, and the indentation diameter (d) was measured.

4) Results and Discussion

4.1 Brinell hardness calculation

HB = 2F / [ π · D · ( D − √(D² − d²) ) ]

F: load = 62.5 kgf  ·  D: ball diameter = 2.5 mm  ·  d: indentation diameter (mm)  ·  HB in kgf/mm²

4.2 Evaluation

In 7xxx alloys the precipitation sequence is αss → GP zones → η′ (metastable) → η (MgZn2, stable). The as-quenched sample has the lowest hardness (79.54 HB) because the solute is still dissolved. After 1 h the hardness rises to 109.50 HB as fine, fully coherent GP zones form; their strain fields hinder dislocation glide. The maximum hardness, 130.95 HB, is reached at 3 h (+64.6 %), when fine semi-coherent η′ particles are densely distributed and dislocations must cut through them. Over-aging starts after 3 h: hardness falls to 124.24 HB at 6 h and 94.95 HB at 12 h, because the particles coarsen and turn into incoherent η. Dislocations then bypass them by the Orowan mechanism at a lower stress. Unlike precipitation hardening, dispersion hardening uses insoluble, incoherent particles (e.g. Al2O3) that do not coarsen, so it keeps strength at high temperature.

The curve has the typical shape reported for heat-treatable Al alloys [1–3]. Handbook values for 7075 are about 60 HB (O) and 150 HB (T6, aged at ~120 °C) [4]. Our peak (131 HB) is lower and earlier because aging at 200 °C makes precipitation faster but the precipitates coarser.

PH stainless steels: the same principle is used in 17-4 PH steel. After solution treatment at ~1040 °C, fine Cu-rich particles precipitate in the martensite during aging. H900 (482 °C, 1 h) gives peak hardness, while H1150 (621 °C, 4 h) is deliberately over-aged for better toughness and corrosion resistance [5].

5) Conclusion

Aging at 200 °C raised the hardness of the 7xxx Al alloy from 79.54 HB to a maximum of 130.95 HB at 3 h (+64.6 %), due to coherent GP zones and semi-coherent η′ precipitates. After 3 h the alloy over-aged (94.95 HB at 12 h) as the precipitates coarsened into incoherent η (MgZn2).
