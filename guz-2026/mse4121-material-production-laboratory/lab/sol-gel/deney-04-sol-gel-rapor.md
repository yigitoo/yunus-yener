# Experiment 4: The Sol-Gel Technique – Lab Report

**Name:** Yunus Yener  **Student No:** 2205C046  **Group-5**

## 1) Aim

This experiment aims to synthesize SiO₂ (silica) nanopowder by the sol-gel method, using TEOS as the silica precursor and a base/fluoride catalyst. The sol is also used to produce thin coatings by dip coating and spin coating. Then the effect of silica content on wettability (contact angle) and on viscosity (Ostwald viscometer) is examined.

## 2) Materials and Devices

- TEOS (Si(OC₂H₅)₄), ethanol, distilled water
- Stock catalyst solution: NH₄F + NH₄OH
- PVA, Triton X-100, the SiO₂ powder we synthesized
- Glass slides, FTO/ITO glass, graphite-coated paper, nanofiber paper
- Magnetic stirrer, beakers, dropper, precision balance, fume hood
- Drying oven, calcination furnace, laser pointer
- Spin coater, Ostwald viscometer, stopwatch

## 3) Experimental Procedure

### 3.1) Sol Preparation and Gelation
- Solution I: 3 mL TEOS and 5 mL ethanol were mixed under magnetic stirring in the fume hood.
- Solution II: 3 mL distilled water and 5 mL ethanol were mixed.
- Stock solution: 1.9 g NH₄F and 23 mL NH₄OH were dissolved in 100 mL distilled water.
- Solution II was added dropwise to Solution I while stirring. This started the hydrolysis of the alkoxide.
- The stock solution was added in portions of 3 drops, with waiting time between portions. When a coating started to form on the glass slide (dip test), the addition was stopped. In total, 12 drops were added.
- Ethanol is the common solvent for TEOS and water. Water starts the hydrolysis. NH₄OH and F⁻ act as catalysts and speed up the reaction (base-catalysed Stöber route [9]).

### 3.2) Dip Coating and Tyndall Effect
- Before full gelation, a glass slide was dipped into the sol and slowly withdrawn. This gave a thin silica layer on the slide.
- After gelation, a laser beam was passed through the gel and through a beaker of distilled water placed next to it.

### 3.3) Drying and Calcination
- The wet gel was weighed and then dried at 100 °C for 24 h to remove water and alcohol.
- It was then calcined at 500 °C for 1 h to remove residual organics, which gave the SiO₂ nanopowder.
- The sample was weighed after each step.

### 3.4) Spin Coating
- Two PVA–SiO₂ suspensions were prepared, with low SiO₂ (0.5 g) and high SiO₂ (1 g). Triton X-100 was used as the surfactant.
- For each test, 3 drops of suspension were placed on FTO/ITO glass and spun with the parameters in Table 2.

### 3.5) Contact Angle
- One drop of distilled water was placed on each of four surfaces: the high-silica coating, the low-silica coating, graphite paper and nanofiber paper.
- The droplet shapes were compared.

### 3.6) Ostwald Viscometer
- The viscometer was filled up to mark C. Using the bulb, the liquid was drawn above mark A.
- The time for the meniscus to fall from A to B was measured.
- Distilled water was used as the reference. The same volume (5 mL) was used for every liquid.
- Each density was found by weighing 5 mL of the liquid (ρ = m/V).

**Table 4. Ostwald viscometer data (V = 5 mL = 5 cm³)**

| Liquid | m (g) | ρ (g/cm³) | t (s) | η (mPa·s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

$$\eta = \eta_0 \frac{\rho\, t}{\rho_0\, t_0}$$

- η = dynamic viscosity of the sample (mPa·s)
- ρ = density of the sample (g/cm³)
- t = flow time of the sample (s)
- Index 0 = reference (distilled water)

Calculations:
- Low SiO₂: η = 0.89 × (1.02586 × 8.06) / (1 × 1.45) = **5.08 mPa·s**, so η/η₀ ≈ 5.70
- High SiO₂: η = 0.89 × (1.02664 × 8.34) / (1 × 1.45) = **5.26 mPa·s**, so η/η₀ ≈ 5.90

Unit check: g/cm³ and s cancel in the ratio, so η keeps the unit of η₀ (mPa·s).

## 5) Discussion and Evaluation of the Results (Questions)

**Q1 – Mass loss.**
- Most of the mass (94.72 %) was lost during drying. The wet gel is mostly liquid (water, ethanol and the ethanol released by hydrolysis and condensation) held inside the silica network.
- During calcination a further 31.60 % was lost. This comes from residual organics (–OC₂H₅ groups), bound water, and Si–OH groups condensing into Si–O–Si.
- The yield (~49 %) is below the theoretical 0.81 g because part of the sol was used for dip coating and some material stayed in the beaker and the crucible.

**Q2 – Tyndall effect.**
- The laser beam was invisible in distilled water but clearly visible in the gel. The colloidal SiO₂ particles in the gel are about the size of the light's wavelength, so they scatter the light in all directions.
- A true solution such as water has no such particles, so the beam passes through without being seen. This shows the product is a colloidal system.

**Q3 – Structure of the SiO₂ powder.**
- The powder is expected to be amorphous. It is made of randomly connected SiO₄ tetrahedra with no long-range order.
- Sol-gel silica normally stays amorphous after heat treatment at 500 °C. It crystallizes (for example to cristobalite) only at much higher temperatures [1].
- *(Draw crystalline SiO₂ and amorphous SiO₂ side by side.)*

**Q4 – Spinning time vs. speed.**
- Time had no visible effect. The reason is that the film thins very quickly in the first seconds.
- For a viscous liquid on a rotating disk [3]:

$$h(t) = \frac{h_0}{\sqrt{1 + \dfrac{4\rho\omega^2 h_0^2 t}{3\eta}}}$$

  - h = film thickness (m)
  - h₀ = initial thickness (m)
  - ω = angular speed (rad/s)
  - ρ = density (kg/m³)
  - η = viscosity (Pa·s)
  - t = time (s)
- At the same time, the solvent evaporates and the viscosity of the PVA film rises quickly, so thinning effectively stops after a few seconds. As a result, the difference between 5 s and 10 s is very small.
- The final thickness depends mainly on the spin speed, roughly h ∝ ω^(−1/2) to ω^(−2/3) [3, 4]. The speed is therefore the controlling parameter.
- Our speeds (500–800 rpm) are also far below the typical ~3000 rpm, so the films stayed thick and uneven.

**Q5 – How to make a superhydrophobic silica surface.**
- More silica made the surface more hydrophilic. Silica is covered with polar Si–OH (silanol) groups, which raise the surface energy and attract water.
- The Young equation [2] describes the contact angle:

$$\gamma_{SV} = \gamma_{SL} + \gamma_{LV}\cos\theta$$

- A superhydrophobic surface (θ > 150°, sliding angle < 10°, i.e. the lotus effect) needs two things:
  1. **Low surface energy.** The Si–OH groups must be replaced by non-polar groups through silanization, using fluoroalkylsilanes (FAS) or methylsilanes (e.g. HMDS) [7].
  2. **Rough surface on the micro/nano scale.** The SiO₂ nanoparticles themselves provide this roughness.
- With both, air is trapped under the drop (Cassie–Baxter state [6]). Roughness amplifies the hydrophobicity of the modified surface (Wenzel [5]). Rain drops then roll off and carry dirt away.

**Q6 – Viscosity.**
- Viscosity rose from 5.08 to 5.26 mPa·s (~3.6 %) as the SiO₂ content increased.
- More solid particles disturb the flow and increase internal friction. The SiO₂ surfaces also form hydrogen bonds with the PVA chains.
- The viscosity of the suspension affects the coating thickness: a more viscous suspension gives a thicker film.
- Possible errors:
  - The stopwatch reaction time (the reference time of 1.45 s is very short).
  - Room temperature, which was not controlled.
  - Using ρ₀ = 1 g/cm³ and η₀ = 0.89 mPa·s instead of the values at 25 °C (0.997 g/cm³ and 0.8909 mPa·s) [8].
